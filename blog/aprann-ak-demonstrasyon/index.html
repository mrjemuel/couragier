How to Learn from a Step-by-Step Demonstration

Everything seems clear while you watch a lesson. The instructor clicks in the right place, gets the expected result, and moves on. When you open the tool yourself, you might get stuck on a decision the video went through quickly. Which column should you select? What should you check before moving on? A demonstration that explains why each step matters can help you make those decisions yourself.

Watch the decision behind the action

In a photography demonstration, the instructor might keep the phone and the mug in place and move only the lamp. You can see what changed and observe the effect. Before the instructor moves the lamp again, pause and predict which way the shadow will go. Resume the video and compare your prediction with what happens.

The same approach works for a spreadsheet lesson. If the instructor sorts expenses by date, watch how they select the whole table. After sorting, check that each amount is still on the same row as its description. That detail gives you a way to check your own work later.

Repeat the example, then change one thing

Keep the demonstration nearby the first time you try. Pause after an important step, do it yourself, and compare your result with what you see on screen. If the results differ, look for the first point where they diverge. That tells you which step to review.

Then try a new example. If the lesson uses a list of expenses, try a list of activities. If it shows you how to light a mug, try a different object. Changing the material gives you a chance to decide which parts of the method still apply and which need adjusting. The What Works Clearinghouse guide recommends alternating worked examples with exercises learners solve on their own.

An exercise you can try today

  1. Create a small table with three columns for Date, Description, and Amount.
  2. Enter five imaginary expenses with dates out of order.
  3. Sort the expenses from oldest to newest.
  4. Check that each amount still matches the correct description and that the total is unchanged.

Tomorrow, try the same exercise with a list of activities. Make your first attempt before opening the demonstration. If you get stuck, write down the decision you need to make. When you return to the video, you will know exactly what to look for.
